# Case study overviews — 2-minute stories

One script per case study, for the overview section at the top of each page.
Each is read in first person, about 2 minutes at a calm pace (~130 words a
minute, with pauses between beats). The narration is also the on-screen transcript, word for word.

Every beat has a **visual**: almost all already exist on the case page, so the
film can be cut from them. Every number is one already confirmed and used on
the page; nothing here is new or rounded up.

The shape is the same each time, so a recruiter watching two learns to follow
the third: **the problem → what I did and how → what changed → what that means
for your team.**

---

## 1. ONTON — "Every organizer was dropping at the same point"

*~270 words · about 2 minutes*

**Beat 1 — The hook** · 0:00–0:15
*Visual: the cover, the Discover screen animating.*

> ONTON lets crypto communities run events on Telegram and reward the people
> who show up with an on-chain badge. But a badge only matters if there's an
> event to attend, and organizers kept giving up before they ever published one.

**Beat 2 — The problem** · 0:15–0:40
*Visual: the old form filling up, 22 fields across four steps, the clock running
to about 7 minutes; then the race against Luma and Partiful.*

> Creating a simple event took four dense steps, more than twenty fields, and a
> warning on almost every screen that your choices were permanent. It took about
> seven minutes. Most organizers dropped at step two or three. Elsewhere, the
> same job takes under a minute.

**Beat 3 — What I heard** · 0:40–0:58
*Visual: the two interview quotes, Isabel and Malena.*

> I interviewed five organizers, and they told me the same story in two halves:
> they were asked for things they didn't need, and then asked again, every single
> week, because nothing carried over.

**Beat 4 — What I did, and how** · 0:58–1:30
*Visual: the 22 fields sorting into kept, moved and removed; then the new flow
filling one event card.*

> I was the only designer, working with the founder and four engineers. I didn't
> delete what mattered; I demoted it. Four steps became one. Nine fields stayed
> up front, eleven moved into Advanced, two were automated away. Defaults now
> stick, events can be duplicated, and there's one confirmation at the end
> instead of a warning on every screen. I built the components it runs on, and
> kept iterating on live analytics after launch.

**Beat 5 — The impact** · 1:30–1:50
*Visual: the four result cards, then the hundred dots, fifteen of them lit.*

> Completion went from twenty percent to eighty-one. Seven minutes became one.
> Four in ten events are now made by duplicating or repeating one. And only fifteen percent of organizers
> ever opened Advanced, proof that almost no one needed what the old flow forced
> on everyone. The product grew from 87 to over 1,500 daily users.

**Beat 6 — The value** · 1:50–2:00
*Visual: the new flow landing in the organizer's events.*

> I find the step everyone's tripping on, cut it down to what people actually
> need, and stay with it until the numbers move.

## 2. Challenquiz — "A multiplayer game that everyone played alone"

*~265 words · about 2 minutes*

**Beat 1 — The hook** · 0:00–0:15
*Visual: the cover, Lena's four verbs lighting up over the real game.*

> Challenquiz is live trivia on Telegram with something at stake: you pay to take
> a seat, answer ten questions, and the best players in the room win the most.
> It was built as a multiplayer game. On screen, everyone played alone.

**Beat 2 — The problem** · 0:15–0:42
*Visual: the old flow step by step, ending on "please wait".*

> You picked a match from a stack of identical cards, read a six-point rulebook,
> waited in an empty lobby, answered with no opponent in sight, and finished on
> "please wait until other players answer". The best moment of the game, finding
> out whether you won, arrived after most people had already closed the app.

**Beat 3 — What I heard** · 0:42–1:00
*Visual: the three player quotes, then the old list, match and result turning
into the redesign.*

> I tested it with five players. They couldn't tell the matches apart, never saw
> who they were playing against, and left before the result. The other players
> were the whole point of the product, and the interface hid them at every step.

**Beat 4 — What I did, and how** · 1:00–1:35
*Visual: the three room models compared; the answer row moving through its six
states; the result counting up to 294.*

> I was the only designer, with the founder and two developers. I designed the
> game around one player, Lena, and four things she wanted: to open it and play
> fast, to play against someone, to learn something, and to enjoy the result.
> Rooms now feel live without making anyone wait. After every answer you see how
> the room answered. And the result lands the moment you finish, with a
> breakdown of exactly what you earned and why.

**Beat 5 — The impact** · 1:35–1:52
*Visual: the month bar, 535 to 1,451, above the study's before and after.*

> In one month, new players who finished a game nearly tripled, from 535 to
> 1,451. In testing with five more players, time to start halved, every game
> finished cleanly, and satisfaction more than doubled.

**Beat 6 — The value** · 1:52–2:00
*Visual: the prototype, ready to play.*

> I design for the moment people come back for, and I put it back on screen.

## 3. Connect2WOW — "One product for the head office and the camp four hours north"

*~260 words · about 2 minutes*

**Beat 1 — The hook** · 0:00–0:18
*Visual: the cover, the module index driving the real prototype screens.*

> Connect2WOW was an ERP for Canada's oil and gas industry, used by an HR manager
> at a desk in Calgary and a welder two weeks into a rotation on a remote site.
> One product had to make sense at both ends of that line.

**Beat 2 — The problem** · 0:18–0:42
*Visual: six loose tools drifting apart, then flying into one product.*

> Payroll lived in spreadsheets, incidents on paper, certificates in a shared
> drive, approvals in email and the crew in a group chat. None of it talked to
> the rest, and the people furthest from the office were the most cut off. And
> with three designers on one product, without a system it would become three
> products.

**Beat 3 — What I did, and how** · 0:42–1:25
*Visual: the six personas on the line from head office to site; the four layers
stacking; the camera pulling back from one status pill to a whole work order.*

> I was one of three product designers, under a design director. We started
> from research, built personas from head office to remote site, and tested every
> module against one question: does it own its own records and work? Seven
> candidates became four modules: HR, safety, training and engineering, with
> everything personal moved into one workspace. Underneath, we built the system
> together: 65 component sets, 370 icons, and one rule for every alert, so an
> expired certificate looks the same everywhere it appears.

**Beat 4 — The honest part** · 1:25–1:45
*Visual: the working prototype, switching personas.*

> The product shut down before it launched, so there's no adoption to show.
> What there is, is a working system, and a lesson I now bring to every
> project: agreement in the team isn't validation. Put it in front of a real
> operator first.

**Beat 5 — The value** · 1:45–2:00
*Visual: the prototype at full size.*

> When a product is too big for one person to hold in their head, I find its
> structure, and I build the system that keeps a whole team designing it as one.

## 4. WOW design system — "Three designers, one library"

*~250 words · about 2 minutes*

**Beat 1 — The hook** · 0:00–0:15
*Visual: the cover, the wall of real components drifting past.*

> WOW is the design system behind Connect2WOW. Three of us were designing one
> enterprise product at the same time, and the library was the only thing
> keeping it from turning into three.

**Beat 2 — The problem** · 0:15–0:35
*Visual: "spot the difference", three versions of the same approval card with
their mismatches ringed.*

> Without shared rules, the same approval card came out three ways: different
> corners, different buttons, different words for the same action. Engineers had
> to guess which one was right, and every guess became another one-off.

**Beat 3 — What I did, and how** · 0:35–1:20
*Visual: the four flip cards (Carbon, Material, Polaris, Atlassian); the four
principles switching on; a button built from tokens; the periodic table of 50
components.*

> Before drawing anything, we took apart four mature systems, Carbon, Material,
> Polaris and Atlassian, to see how they handle feedback, where enterprise
> products hold together or fall apart. Then we set four rules: structure
> decides, not taste; colour carries meaning or nothing; every component ships
> with every state; one name for one thing, in Figma, in code and in
> conversation. Over fifteen months that became fifty components and 370 icons.
> We each researched our own components, decided together every day, and handed
> over to the engineers every week.

**Beat 4 — The impact** · 1:20–1:40
*Visual: the light and dark bento re-skinning; the before-and-after slider of
the 2026 revisit.*

> By the end, nobody could tell whose hand was on which screen. A whole dark
> theme came from new token values, without redrawing a single component. And
> when I returned to it this year, I bound 771 colour values to tokens without
> changing a pixel.

**Beat 5 — The value** · 1:40–2:00
*Visual: the handover, the button's spec passing to the engineers' prototype.*

> It never reached users, so its rules were agreed, not tested, and next time
> I'd test the boldest parts first. What it shows is what I bring to a team: a
> system that lets several designers and engineers ship as one, faster, without
> arguing about the basics.

## Notes for production

- **Voice:** first person, your own voice if you can record it; it's the most
  convincing thing in the section. Read slowly; the timings assume pauses
  between beats.
- **Transcript:** show the narration as written, beat by beat, so it can be read
  without sound and skimmed by a recruiter in under a minute.
- **One gap:** Connect2WOW's "What I did" beat says "we" because the modules were
  split across the team. If you tell me which modules you led, that line can say
  "I" and sell harder.
